Some people want to receive IV therapy but feel anxious about needles or have previously needed several attempts to place an IV.

Tell the nurse about these concerns when booking or before treatment begins. Knowing about your previous experiences can help the nurse consider your position, the insertion site, and how to make the procedure more manageable.

## What Should You Tell the Nurse Before IV Therapy?

Let the nurse know if you have experienced any of the following:

- Strong anxiety or a racing heartbeat when seeing needles
- Dizziness, nausea, or fainting during a blood test or IV
- Veins that have previously been difficult to find
- Several insertion attempts during a previous procedure
- An arm or location where IV placement is usually easier
- Breast or lymph-node surgery, dialysis, or vascular treatment
- A medical reason to limit your fluid intake

A history of difficult IV placement and veins that are difficult to see or feel can help identify people who may have difficult intravenous access. A [systematic review published on PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34789023/) describes these as important factors in assessing difficult IV access.

## What Can You Do If You Are Afraid of Needles?

There is no need to hide your anxiety. Tell the nurse if you prefer not to see the needle, would like the nurse to talk to you during the procedure, or have previously felt faint.

The following strategies may help:

- Look away from the needle and equipment
- Breathe slowly and comfortably
- Talk with the nurse
- Listen to music or use another form of distraction
- Ask whether you can lie down if you are prone to fainting
- Report dizziness, nausea, sweating, or weakness immediately

Guy’s and St Thomas’ NHS Foundation Trust recommends telling healthcare professionals about needle anxiety. It also describes conversation, distraction, and relaxation breathing as possible coping strategies. [Needle phobia and overcoming your fear](https://www.guysandstthomas.nhs.uk/print-this-resource?mode=normal&nodes%5B4842%5D=4842)

## How Can You Prepare If Your Veins Are Difficult to Find?

If you do not have a fluid restriction, drink your usual amount of water and avoid becoming dehydrated before your appointment.

MedlinePlus notes that drinking water and staying warm may make veins easier to find during a blood test. These steps may also be helpful when preparing for IV placement. [MedlinePlus: How to Cope with Medical Test Anxiety](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/how-to-cope-with-medical-test-anxiety/)

However, do not increase your fluid intake without advice if you have heart disease, kidney disease, or another condition that requires fluid restriction. Follow the instructions given by your physician.

Wear clothing with sleeves that can be raised comfortably above the elbow. You can also tell the nurse which arm or location has worked well during previous blood tests or IV treatments.

## What If You Feel Pain During IV Placement?

A brief stinging sensation may occur when the needle is inserted. However, tell the nurse immediately if you experience:

- Strong or increasing pain
- A burning sensation
- Numbness or tingling
- Swelling
- A feeling of pressure around the IV site

You should also speak up if your anxiety becomes difficult to manage. The nurse can check the IV site and your condition before deciding whether treatment can continue.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I Receive IV Therapy If My Veins Are Difficult to Find?

Possibly. Vein visibility and access vary from person to person and may also change from day to day. IV placement may take longer, require more than one attempt, or occasionally be postponed depending on the situation.

### Can the IV Be Inserted While I Am Lying Down?

Tell the clinic if you have previously fainted or feel more comfortable lying down. The nurse can review the treatment location and available space before deciding how to proceed.

### Can I Use a Numbing Cream?

The products available and the correct timing differ between clinics. Do not apply a numbing product without instructions, as it may not be suitable for every person or procedure. Ask the clinic before your appointment.

## Conclusion

If you are afraid of needles or have previously had difficult IV access, tell the nurse before treatment begins.

Information about previous insertion attempts, fainting, the arm that usually works best, and any relevant medical history can help the nurse prepare. If you do not have a fluid restriction, maintaining your usual hydration and staying warm may also be helpful.
