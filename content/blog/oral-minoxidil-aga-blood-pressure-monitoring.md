Oral minoxidil is sometimes prescribed as part of treatment for androgenetic alopecia, or AGA. It was originally developed as a blood-pressure medication, and its effects on hair growth later led to its use in hair-loss treatment.

At Pitonne, the standard AGA treatment set includes oral minoxidil 5 mg and dutasteride when medically appropriate. Before prescribing treatment, the physician reviews your blood pressure, medical history, and current medication.

## Is Oral Minoxidil Approved for AGA?

Oral minoxidil is not approved in Japan specifically for AGA treatment. When prescribed for hair loss, it is used off-label.

The Japanese Dermatological Association’s 2017 guideline did not recommend oral minoxidil at that time because its hair-growth benefits and cardiovascular risks had not been adequately evaluated.

More research on low-dose oral minoxidil has since been published. However, the appropriate dose and monitoring plan are not the same for every patient. Individual medical assessment remains important.

[Japanese Dermatological Association: Guidelines for the Treatment of Male and Female Pattern Hair Loss](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/dermatol/127/13/127_2763/_article/-char/ja/)

## What Should You Tell the Physician Before Treatment?

During your online consultation, tell the physician about:

- Your usual blood-pressure readings
- Dizziness or previous fainting
- Palpitations or an irregular heartbeat
- Heart, kidney, or liver conditions
- Previous swelling of the hands, feet, ankles, or face
- Prescription medication
- Over-the-counter medication and supplements
- Other AGA treatments
- Previous reactions to medication

It is especially important to report blood-pressure medication and other drugs that may affect blood pressure. Do not stop another medicine or change your oral minoxidil dose without consulting the prescribing physician.

## What Effects Have Been Reported?

A multicenter study examined 1,404 patients who used low-dose oral minoxidil for at least three months. The most frequently reported effect was hypertrichosis, meaning increased facial or body hair.

Reported effects included:

- Hypertrichosis: 15.1%
- Lightheadedness: 1.7%
- Fluid retention or swelling: 1.3%
- Tachycardia: 0.9%
- Headache: 0.4%
- Swelling around the eyes: 0.3%

A total of 1.7% of patients discontinued treatment because of adverse effects.

However, this was a retrospective observational study without a control group. The likelihood of experiencing these effects may vary according to the dose and the patient’s health condition.

[PubMed: Safety of low-dose oral minoxidil for hair loss](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33639244/)

## Do Blood Pressure, Heart Rate, and Weight Need Daily Monitoring?

The same monitoring schedule is not required for every patient.

A study published in 2025 examined blood pressure, heart rate, weight, kidney function, and liver function in patients taking low-dose oral minoxidil. These measurements remained generally stable in asymptomatic patients whose baseline results were normal.

The researchers concluded that routine monitoring may not be necessary for every asymptomatic patient with normal baseline measurements. They recommended continued monitoring for patients with impaired kidney function.

This single study does not establish a universal monitoring schedule. Follow the physician’s instructions regarding home measurements, laboratory testing, and follow-up consultations.

[PubMed: The Requirement to Monitor Low-dose Oral Minoxidil](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41346548/)

## What Symptoms Should You Monitor?

After starting oral minoxidil, watch for:

- Significant dizziness when standing
- Palpitations or an increased resting heart rate
- Swelling of the ankles, hands, or face
- Rapid weight gain over a short period
- Headaches that interfere with daily activities
- Shortness of breath
- Pain in the chest, arm, or shoulder

If swelling, palpitations, or persistent dizziness develops, contact the prescribing physician rather than waiting until your next scheduled consultation.

Chest pain, severe breathing difficulty, or fainting may require urgent medical attention.

The official US prescribing information for oral minoxidil also lists rapid weight gain, swelling, an increased heart rate, breathing difficulty, and chest pain as warning signs. However, this information was written for the treatment of severe hypertension and does not establish that these symptoms occur at the same frequency with AGA doses.

[DailyMed: Minoxidil Tablets](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=f76f6a05-11f8-48a6-98a3-b1407c228089)

## When Should Results Be Evaluated?

AGA treatment should not be judged after only a few weeks. Hair changes may take several months to become noticeable.

Stopping treatment may allow the condition to change again. If you do not notice improvement or are concerned about an adverse effect, do not increase, reduce, or stop the medication without consulting the physician.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I Take Oral Minoxidil If I Have Low Blood Pressure?

Tell the physician about your usual blood pressure and any history of dizziness or fainting. Additional assessment may be required depending on your blood pressure, symptoms, and current medication.

### Should I Stop Taking It If Swelling Develops?

Do not make the decision based only on your own judgment. Contact the prescribing physician and report where the swelling occurred, whether your weight changed, and whether you have breathing difficulty.

### Can I Take Two Tablets If I Miss a Dose?

Do not take two doses together. Follow the instructions provided with your prescription or ask the physician or pharmacist how to handle a missed dose.

### Can Oral Minoxidil Be Taken With Blood-Pressure Medication?

The combination may affect blood pressure. Tell the physician about every medication you use, including blood-pressure medication, before treatment is prescribed.

## Conclusion

Oral minoxidil is sometimes prescribed for AGA, but blood pressure, heart rate, swelling, medical history, and current medication should be considered before treatment.

Tell the physician if you have low blood pressure, heart or kidney disease, previous swelling, or take medication that affects blood pressure. Monitoring and follow-up should be planned according to your baseline health and any symptoms that develop.
