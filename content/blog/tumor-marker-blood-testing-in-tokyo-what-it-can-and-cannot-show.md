People considering tumor marker blood testing in Tokyo may want to understand what these tests can show and whether they can detect cancer.

Tumor markers are substances that may be found at higher levels in some people with cancer. However, they can also change because of non-cancerous conditions. A high result does not automatically mean that someone has cancer, and a normal result does not rule out every type of cancer.

For this reason, tumor marker results should be interpreted together with symptoms, medical history, physical examination, imaging, and other tests when needed.

## What Is a Tumor Marker?

A tumor marker is a substance found in the blood or other body fluids that may provide information related to cancer or another condition.

According to the National Cancer Institute, tumor markers may be used to help with diagnosis, treatment planning, monitoring, or checking whether cancer has returned after treatment.

However, tumor marker tests are not suitable as a stand-alone test for diagnosing cancer. Non-cancerous conditions can also cause an elevated result, and some people with cancer may not have an elevated marker.

[National Cancer Institute: Tumor Markers](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/diagnosis-staging/diagnosis/tumor-markers-fact-sheet)

## What Can CEA Testing Show?

CEA, or carcinoembryonic antigen, is a tumor marker that may be associated with certain cancers, including colorectal cancer.

CEA levels can also be affected by non-cancerous conditions and lifestyle factors. An elevated CEA level does not identify the exact cause by itself.

CEA may be more useful when it is interpreted alongside:

- Current symptoms
- Previous CEA results
- Imaging findings
- A known cancer diagnosis
- Treatment history
- Other blood tests

A single CEA result should not be used to confirm or exclude cancer without further medical review.

## What Can CA19-9 Testing Show?

CA19-9 is a marker that may be associated with certain conditions involving the digestive system, including some pancreatic and biliary cancers.

An elevated CA19-9 result does not automatically mean that cancer is present. Non-cancerous conditions can also affect the result.

If CA19-9 is outside the expected range, a physician may consider the result together with symptoms, liver and pancreatic tests, imaging, and other relevant information.

The result alone cannot confirm the location or cause of a health problem.

## What Can PSA Testing Show?

PSA, or prostate-specific antigen, is a marker related to the prostate.

PSA levels may be used as part of a discussion about prostate health. However, a raised PSA level does not always mean prostate cancer. Benign prostate conditions and other factors can also affect the result.

The National Cancer Institute explains that PSA testing has potential benefits and harms, including false-positive results and overdiagnosis.

Men considering PSA testing should discuss their age, symptoms, family history, previous PSA results, and personal preferences with an appropriate healthcare professional.

[National Cancer Institute: What Cancer Screening Tests Check for Cancer?](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/screening/screening-tests)

## What Can CA125 Testing Show?

CA125 is a marker that may be associated with ovarian cancer and some other conditions.

A raised CA125 result does not confirm ovarian cancer. Levels can also change for reasons that are not cancer-related.

When CA125 is measured, the result may need to be considered together with:

- Symptoms
- Menstrual and reproductive history
- Physical examination
- Imaging
- Previous test results
- Other blood tests

A CA125 test cannot replace an appropriate medical evaluation for pelvic pain, unusual bleeding, abdominal swelling, or other concerning symptoms.

## What Can Tumor Marker Testing Not Confirm?

Tumor marker blood testing can provide information, but it cannot answer every cancer-related question.

The result alone cannot always:

- Confirm that cancer is present
- Rule out cancer
- Identify the exact location of a disease
- Determine the stage of cancer
- Replace imaging
- Replace a biopsy
- Explain the cause of every symptom
- Confirm that a person is cancer-free
- Replace recommended cancer screening
- Determine treatment without further evaluation

The NCI notes that tumor markers generally do not work well as a general screening method. They may not identify every person with cancer and may also suggest cancer in someone who does not have it.

[National Cancer Institute: Tumor Markers](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/diagnosis-staging/diagnosis/tumor-markers-fact-sheet)

## Should You Have Tumor Marker Testing Without Symptoms?

The decision depends on your health information and the purpose of testing.

If you have symptoms such as unexplained weight loss, persistent pain, unusual bleeding, a change in bowel habits, prolonged cough, abdominal swelling, or another concerning change, arrange an appropriate medical consultation rather than relying only on a private blood test.

If you have no symptoms, tumor marker testing should not be treated as a replacement for recommended screening programs. Ask a healthcare professional which screening tests are appropriate for your age, sex, family history, and personal risk factors.

## What Should You Tell the Clinic Before Testing?

Before tumor marker blood testing, tell the clinic about:

- Current symptoms
- When the symptoms started
- Previous blood test results
- Previous imaging or medical examinations
- A past or current cancer diagnosis
- Previous cancer treatment
- Family history
- Prescription medications
- Over-the-counter medicines
- Vitamins and supplements
- Recent infections or procedures
- Pregnancy or reproductive health information, when relevant

If you are having testing because of a specific concern, explain that concern clearly. This helps the nurse or physician understand what information should be reviewed after the test.

Do not delay urgent medical care while waiting for a private blood test.

## What Happens After the Test?

Once the results are available, a nurse or physician should review them with you.

Depending on the results and your health information, the next step may include:

- No further testing
- Repeating the blood test
- Reviewing another blood marker
- Imaging
- A consultation with a specialist
- A biopsy or other diagnostic test
- Monitoring over time

An elevated result may require additional testing, but it does not automatically mean that cancer is present.

A result within the expected range may be reassuring, but it does not necessarily explain every symptom or rule out every disease.
